AX5 · 01
The problem I kept running into
Field notes

Manufacturing knowledge arrives after the design is fixed

Almost every hardware product, from a scooter to a car interior, follows the same path: brief, sketch, concept, CAD, engineering review, supplier quotes, tooling. Designers shape the first half, where form and intent are set. Manufacturing knowledge arrives in the second half, when an engineer or a vendor first sees the CAD. By then every constraint they find becomes rework.

I have seen this from both ends: first in automotive service, where design decisions showed up as field failures, then in design, where they showed up as rework. At Yulu we spent six weeks designing a battery bucket for a vehicle's battery compartment. The tooling vendor then told us it could not be moulded, because of undercuts nobody had flagged. The redesign pushed back the launch. Those undercuts could have been caught at the sketch stage.

Small mass-produced consumer electronics take 9 to 12 months from concept to production (MIT). An injection mould quoted at 8 to 12 weeks can become 14 to 16 once DFM questions, approvals and trials begin (QualityInspection).

Making is not the slow part. Deciding is.

The loop I saw on project after project

  1. A designer explores concepts and chooses a direction.
  2. The form is modelled in CAD and sent to an engineer or supplier.
  3. The supplier replies: too complex, or too expensive. Undercuts, thin or uneven walls, missing draft.
  4. The design goes back for changes, often losing what made it distinctive.

AI widens the gap. Designers can now generate unusual forms in minutes, but manufacturing feedback still arrives weeks later, from a supplier.

AI also loses the designer's intent. Designers already use AI image tools, but each pass redraws the sketch: proportions drift, details change, and the designer's own hand turns into a generic AI style. Axis5 locks the design and the viewpoint before any AI pass, so only what the designer asked for changes. Studio Twin goes further and keeps the designer's way of drawing.

The tools that exist start too late. Rule-based DFM checkers assume finished CAD and an engineering team. Marketplaces return a quote, not a decision. Nobody is critiquing the design at the sketch stage.

The cycle is split across tools. Brief, sketch, CAD, DFM and quotes each live somewhere else, and context is lost at every handoff.

Nine steps, one workspace
Recorded on the live product

Nine steps carry the journey

01 / 09 · DEFINE

Brief

The need
A product that answers the wrong problem is wasted, however well it is drawn. Getting the brief and the problem statement right decides what every later choice is judged against.
What it does
Drafts a structured brief with the designer: product, user, situation, need, measurable criteria and constraints. Anything Axis5 adds is labelled as its suggestion.
Why it matters
Every later critique, direction and check is judged against it, so feedback becomes a test rather than an opinion.

In a recorded sessionA compact two-door electric coupe for city commuting was judged on measurable criteria, such as a length under 4.2 m and a 350 km range. Axis5's own additions were labelled as suggestions.

02 / 09 · SKETCH

Critique

The need
Sketching is how an idea becomes something others can see and judge. Many people have strong ideas but not the sketching skill to show them, so the idea is dropped or misread. Constructive critique helps them correct the sketch and say what they mean.
What it does
Reads the sketch, identifies the product and view, scores six design criteria against the brief and names the next moves.
Why it matters
Feedback arrives while the design is still a sketch, when a change costs a redraw.

In a recorded sessionThe coupe sketch scored 5.8/10, studio band "Developing". Perspective and wheel alignment were named as the main issues, with a wider stance and longer wheelbase as the next move.

03 / 09 · SKETCH

Explore

The need
Exploration is the most important stage of developing any idea: it is where the first thought meets better alternatives. Rushing it locks in the first idea.
What it does
Proposes ten directions within the brief. Each states what changes, what stays and how it serves the brief.
Why it matters
Exploration stays tied to the brief rather than producing random variations, and the choice stays with the designer.

In a recorded sessionFrom a coupe sketch, direction 4, wide wheel-arch extensions, was carried forward and rendered.

04 / 09 · SKETCH

Enhance and Render

The need
A rough sketch only convinces people who read sketches. A clean redraw, a realistic render and the same design seen from other views let engineers, managers and buyers judge the idea too.
What it does
Enhance redraws the sketch cleanly, keeping its proportions and character, with the original beside it. Two quick questions, style and colour, then set up a realistic render.
Why it matters
A designer needs to see an idea clearly to judge it, and to show it to people who do not read rough sketches.

In a recorded sessionOne message, "give me a side view of this car", turned the hatchback render to a side profile with the design unchanged.

Studio Twin

The designer's own hand, kept

A designer uploads 3 to 15 of their own sketches, or a portfolio link, and Axis5 describes how they draw: line quality, stroke energy and shading habits. Once the designer confirms it, Enhance and Render lean toward their hand instead of a generic AI style.

An approximation, not a clone. It can be edited, relearned or switched off at any time.

05 / 09 · SKETCH

CMF

The need
Colour, material and finish increasingly decide how a product is perceived, and new materials and processes keep widening the choice. Designers need to try them on their own idea early, not leave them to the end.
What it does
Changes a design's colour, material and finish while keeping its form. The direction can be this year's researched trend for the category, the designer's own words, or a reference image.
Why it matters
CMF shapes how a product is perceived and priced, and every finish implies a process, a supplier and a cost.

In a recorded sessionWarm Minimalism 2026 (warm neutrals, soft-touch matte, brushed-metal accents) was applied to the headphone sketch. The form stayed as drawn.

06 / 09 · SKETCH → PARTS

Parts

The need
A designer who knows how many parts a product needs, and where they split, makes better decisions about form, cost and assembly. Without a tool, that awareness arrives only when a CAD engineer models the part.
What it does
Breaks the sketch into the parts it would be made from, each with a function, likely material and process, then explodes it into clickable parts. Concerns arrive as questions, not verdicts.
Why it matters
This is where a sketch first meets manufacturing, before any CAD exists.

In a recorded sessionThe doors came back as a steel inner structure with an aluminium or composite skin, stamped, hemmed and bolted.

Hatchback 20 parts · 15 drawn · 5 hidden
07 / 09 · PRODUCTION

DFM

The need
Manufacturability decides whether a design ever reaches production. Most designers never see a toolmaker's checklist, so draft, wall thickness and undercuts are left for someone else to find.
What it does
On a STEP file, identifies the part and process and asks only what the geometry cannot say. It checks draft, walls, undercuts, sink risk and edges, marks each issue on the part and proposes a combined fix.
Why it matters
These are the problems a tooling vendor would otherwise find weeks later, like the battery bucket's undercuts.

A real Yulu partThe speedometer's outer casing had shown sink marks in production. Uploaded to Axis5, it was flagged for sink risk across 99.4% of its surface, the most serious of nine issues. Since then, the team runs new plastic parts through Axis5 before tooling.

Manufacturability 33/100 · 9 issues
08 / 09 · PRODUCTION

Scale

The need
A design is only viable at the cost and volume it will be made at. Quantity, material and location change both the price and the process, yet designers rarely see those numbers while the design can still change.
What it does
Three questions (quantity, hub, material) become a live model of unit cost, tooling, lead time and readiness across Indian manufacturing hubs. Every figure is labelled indicative.
Why it matters
Cost and tooling decisions usually wait weeks for supplier quotes, so design choices are made without knowing what they cost.

In a recorded sessionThe same case at 100,000 units from Peenya: about ₹31 a unit and ₹5.97 lakh of tooling, with Ahmedabad about 9% cheaper.

09 / 09 · PRODUCTION

Quotation

The need
A design becomes a product only when someone can make it, at the right price. Choosing the right supplier and reading the quote well decide whether that happens.
What it does
A Marketplace Bridge drafts a request for quotation from the part and opens it on IndiaMART, Google Maps, JustDial or instant-quote partners. A quote analyser then reviews what comes back.
Why it matters
Small teams rarely know which suppliers to approach, or how to judge the quotes they receive.

In the product demoFor an aluminium part from Peenya, the Marketplace Bridge drafted the inquiry and opened a Google Maps search of die-casting and CNC vendors nearby. A real supplier quote was then uploaded: the analyser flagged missing tooling costs as critical and suggested asking for them as a separate line, with a negotiation script.

AX5 · 04
Spotlight · Product Inclusion Review
Sketch stage

Who cannot use it is decided on the sketch

What it does
Reviews the design against the seven principles of Universal Design, with cited guidelines. It names who would struggle to use the product and why. Accepted changes are drawn onto the sketch, with the original kept for comparison.
Why it matters
Who cannot use a product is a design decision. It is far cheaper to answer on a sketch than after tooling.
Value in the journey
Inclusion becomes part of the design loop rather than a late audit.

In a recorded sessionOver two rounds of accepted changes, the right-hand dispenser moved its bottle into a bottom-loading cabinet and got HOT and COLD marked on its taps. The dispensers also got a window onto the bottle's water line and a red float that rises as the drip tray fills. Where an image model could not draw a change as asked, Axis5 said so.

Going further. A System Deep Dive looks beyond the object to the whole journey of use: finding it, approaching, using, refilling, cleaning and repairing it, and the people who keep it running. A separate Fit & Ergonomics check reviews reach, grip, adjustment and comfort against the brief, cites its references and names what only a mock-up or a trial with real users can settle.

AX5 · 05
What I tried, what failed, what I killed
Decision log

Behind the features: six calls I made

Each feature above went through versions that did not work. These are the calls that shaped them.

01 · Workflow

One conversation, not three modes

Tried
Separate Dream, Build and Scale modes, one for each stage of the journey.
What went wrong
Context was lost at every switch, the same handoff problem Axis5 exists to fix.
What I changed
I killed the modes. One conversation now carries the work, and an orchestrator picks the tool.

02 · Package Check

Measure before believing

Tried
Proportion bands that decided what kind of vehicle a sketch showed.
What went wrong
On a benchmark of 1,099 student sketches and renders, most cars read as vans, and a sports car scored as a pickup.
What I changed
The view now decides the body type, and the bands only give feedback. Three-quarter views are declined, and multi-vehicle sheets ask to be cropped.

03 · Exploded view

Don't let a model guess the parts

Tried
Cutting the finished sketch into parts with an image-segmentation model.
What went wrong
On a drawing where parts touch, it has to guess where one ends, and it added a heavy dependency.
What I changed
Axis5 draws the parts apart first, counts them against the parts list, and says which are still fused.

04 · Style library

My eye over the metric

Tried
Tuning a storyboard style until it matched the reference's measured light and dark.
What went wrong
It scored higher and looked worse. The styles also hard-coded the reference colours, so every sketch came out orange.
What I changed
I locked the richer version by eye, and measurements now only flag drift. Colour is a choice within each style.

05 · Inclusion review

Kill false precision

Tried
A score from 1 to 10 for each Universal Design principle.
What went wrong
A number from a single front sketch was invented precision.
What I changed
Each principle now reads "visible support", "potential barrier" or "needs evidence", with a confidence level.

06 · Market judgment

Commit before you debate

Tried
Inferring the target buyer and price silently, without asking.
What went wrong
A designer cannot argue with a verdict on a price they never committed to.
What I changed
The read asks for intent first. A suggested change edits the sketch before anything is rendered, so the sketch stays the designer's own.

Three rules that run through everything

Each check needs its own evidence

DFM runs only on a CAD file. Package Check declines a three-quarter sketch and asks for a side, front or rear view.

The designer chooses; the system offers

Explore offers ten directions within the brief, and Enhance keeps the original beside the new version.

Uncertainty is labelled where the number appears

Scale says "indicative, not live quotes" beside its figures. Meshes say concept, and market reads say directional.

AX5 · 08
Impact, limits and what comes next
Next experiments

What changed, what is still unproven

What changed for Yulu's team

  • Plastic parts are run through DFM before they reach a toolmaker.
  • Technical questions are discussed against the part itself, with sources.
  • Ideas are explored faster: sketch to directions to render in one sitting.
  • A first dimensioned drawing takes minutes instead of waiting for drafting.
  • Fewer handoffs: design, manufacturability, cost and drawings in one project.

Reported by the team; not yet measured against a baseline.

What this does not show yet

  • Time saved or better designs. No before-and-after on the same kind of task.
  • Reliable engineering results. DFM and Scale have barely been used by others; costs are indicative, not quotes.
  • Lasting use. Most people used Axis5 in one week and did not return.

Three habits behind this work

Looking back over Yulu and Axis5, the same three habits keep showing up. Each one points to where it appears on this page.

01

Start where the cost lands

Most problems I have worked on were found late, by someone downstream. I start where that cost lands and trace it back to the decision that caused it.

Seen inWater in the Miracle 2 floorboard, the battery bucket, the sketch-to-vendor gap. The problem · My role ↑

02

Build something people can test

I would rather put a rough working version in front of people than a polished concept. Real use shows what a screen hides.

Seen inAxis5, first built with AI-assisted coding and refined in college workshops; the phone holder below. The nine steps ↑

03

Say what is not proven

I report the limits as plainly as the results, and turn each gap into a test.

Seen inThe six calls that changed course, and the experiments set against each open question. Six calls · Still unproven ↑

Before Axis5

On Yulu's shared EVs, the off-the-shelf phone holders we bought at ₹500 broke within weeks, and riders skipped vehicles without one. The team treated it as a sourcing problem. I treated it as a product problem, because no holder on the market was built for round-the-clock outdoor shared use. After three prototype rounds, we shipped a holder that outlasted every off-the-shelf option at ₹477 a unit, under the original cap.

With a new team, I would start by learning how decisions are made today, and only then decide what to build.
